An aurora, also known as the northern lights or southern lights, is a natural light 
display in Earth's sky. These shimmering curtains of green, red, or purple light are 
predominantly seen in high-latitude regions around the Arctic and Antarctic. Auroras 
are caused by disturbances in Earth's magnetosphere from the solar wind. This stream 
of charged particles from the Sun interacts with gases in our atmosphere, like oxygen 
and nitrogen.

When these solar particles collide with atmospheric gases, they excite the gas molecules, 
causing them to light up—a process similar to how a neon sign glows. The color of the 
aurora depends on the type of gas and the altitude of the collision. Green, the most 
common color, comes from oxygen about 60 miles up. Red comes from high-altitude oxygen, 
while nitrogen produces blue or purple hues.

Auroras are not unique to Earth. Other planets with magnetic fields and atmospheres, 
like Jupiter and Saturn, also have them. The best places to see Earth's auroras are 
in a ring-shaped zone around the magnetic poles, such as Alaska, Canada, Iceland, Norway, 
and Antarctica. They are most visible on clear, dark nights during periods of high solar 
activity, like during the solar maximum—a period of intense sunspot cycles that occurs 
every 11 years.

Historically, auroras have been interpreted as spiritual omens or dragon fires by various 
cultures. Scientific study began in earnest in the 18th century. Today, they are a major 
tourist attraction and a key subject for space weather research, helping scientists 
understand interactions between the Sun and Earth.
